# Human Adaptive Technology Studio

We research and build technology that adapts to people, rather than expecting people to adapt to it.

## What we mean by Human Adaptive Technology

Most systems behave the same way regardless of who is using them or what is happening around them. Human Adaptive Technology is our term for systems that do three things:

1. **Sense** — pick up signals about a person's state and their context, from sources such as physiological sensors, interaction behaviour, or the surrounding environment.
2. **Interpret** — turn those signals into a meaningful estimate of what is going on. Attention, effort, stress, engagement and capability are examples. None of them are read directly off a sensor.
3. **Adapt** — change what the system does in response, in a way that supports the person.

The third step is the hard one, and the one we care most about. Detecting a state is not useful on its own. The value is in what the system does next, and whether that action genuinely helps.

## Areas of work

The Studio works across a set of connected areas. These describe where our interests lie — not a catalogue of finished components:

- Sensing and physiological computing
- Contextual sensing
- Human-state modelling
- Multimodal AI
- Adaptive decision-making and orchestration
- Conversational and embodied agents
- XR and immersive systems
- Adaptive interfaces
- Digital health and intervention systems
- Evaluation infrastructure and research tooling
- Responsible AI and governance

## How we approach this

**Adaptation should serve the person.** A system that infers something about someone and then acts on it is exercising a kind of authority. We are interested in adaptation that supports wellbeing, capability, agency, accessibility and performance — and sceptical of adaptation that optimises for engagement or compliance.

**Inference is uncertain, and should be treated that way.** Human states are estimated, not measured. A system that treats an estimate as fact will be confidently wrong about someone eventually. The interesting design question is what happens when it is.

**Research software is real software.** Code written to answer a research question still needs to be understandable, reproducible, and safe to hand to the next person.

**Projects may be temporary, but reusable capabilities should accumulate.** Projects end. The genuinely useful parts should not have to be rebuilt each time — though we only generalise something once a second project actually needs it.

## About this organisation

Most repositories here are private. Research code stays private by default until questions of ownership, licensing, ethics, cultural governance, publication and partner obligations have been worked through. That is a deliberate position rather than a lack of interest in open source, and repositories are opened when it is appropriate to open them.

The Studio is at an early stage. Expect this organisation to be sparse for a while.
